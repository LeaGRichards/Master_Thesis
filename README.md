# Master_Thesis

Abstract:
Grey wolf (Canis lupus, Linnaeus, 1758) populations declined drastically across Europe in the 20th century (Zimen & Boitani, 1975). Legal protection measures have helped recolonization and expansion of wolves across Europe, and more specifically in Italy, where the species was strictly protected until a recent change in status (Council of Europe, 2024; European Parliament, 2025). Population connectivity is an important factor in their conservation and correctly identifying ecological corridors to sustain genetic exchanges is of utmost importance. Currently, no landscape-connectivity evaluation exists for the whole of continental Italy, and building such a large model is beyond a preliminary study. Instead, this thesis evaluates what existing published wolf models can reveal about areas of high connectivity that should be further studied to inform wolf conservation measures. I transferred coefficients of three models from two published studies on continental Italy (250,397 km²): a Slovakian resource selection function (Guimarães et al., 2025, non-breeding season model) and a Portuguese habitat suitability model (Rio-Maior et al., 2019, disperser and resident long-movement models). From these models, I built resistance surfaces, modelled connectivity with Least-Cost Path (LCP) analysis (Adriaensen et al., 2003), and compared the resulting areas of interest across models to identify convergent priority areas for future, more rigorous wolf conservation research. High-connectivity areas converge mainly in the Apennines but lack convergence in the Alps contrary to our hypothesis. The limitations of LCP and of model transfer and upscaling warrant caution to these results. Still, the Apennines are areas of interest that I suggest for prioritizing future, statistically robust connectivity research and corridor-protection planning in Italy for wolf conservation measures.
